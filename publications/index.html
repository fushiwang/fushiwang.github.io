Social Vulnerability, Disaster Exposure, and County Economic Recovery

Fushi Wang · Co-authored paper · April 2026

Do storms of comparable intensity impose different economic costs on counties with different levels of social vulnerability? We link county-coded NOAA storm events from 2000–2005 to CDC Social Vulnerability Index measures and county economic outcomes. We use event-level and county-year regressions to distinguish the physical intensity of a storm from its realized economic damage. At comparable intensities, more vulnerable counties experience greater property losses. Realized damage also predicts a stronger unemployment response, with suggestive evidence of weaker recovery over the medium term.

Does Cannabis Policy Displace Alcohol? Evidence from U.S. State-Level Consumption Data, 1994–2023

Fushi Wang and Ajay Raman · April 2026

We examine whether changes in state cannabis policy coincide with changes in the consumption of beer, wine, and spirits. We construct a panel of the 50 states from 1994 through 2023, combining measures of policy implementation with NIAAA apparent consumption data, alcohol excise taxes, and economic controls. Differences across states explain much of the raw association. In the fixed-effects models, pharmaceutical provisions are associated with higher wine consumption and fiscal provisions with lower wine consumption. Lagged specifications and ridge regressions provide little consistent evidence that cannabis policy displaces alcohol consumption overall.

Modeling Extremism and Information: Policy Selection and Digital Platforms

Fushi Wang · December 2024

I extend the Hotelling–Downs spatial voting model to allow the share of informed voters to rise with the extremity of a candidate’s position. Candidates face a trade-off between appealing to the median voter and attracting attention. The model predicts that candidates with less initial visibility have a stronger incentive to move away from the center when exposure responds to extremity. An exploratory analysis pairs posting activity on X with ideological scores for ten highly active members of Congress. The estimated relationship is positive and imprecise. The small, selected sample illustrates how the mechanism might be measured and leaves its empirical magnitude open.

An Introduction to Spectral Graph Theory

Fushi Wang · Co-authored paper · May 2024

This survey examines what the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a graph’s adjacency and Laplacian matrices reveal about the graph itself. Starting from linear algebra and graph theory, we develop the spectral theorem and Rayleigh’s quotient, explain how Wilf’s theorem and Hoffman’s bound relate eigenvalues to graph coloring, and examine the spectrum of a bipartite graph. We conclude with a six-page web graph that illustrates how a random walk with restarts produces PageRank scores from its stationary distribution.

The Impact of Education Attainment on Investment Choices

Fushi Wang, Viroop Konchada, and Devansh Shah · December 2023

We use six IPUMS Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement cross-sections from 2007, 2008, 2010, 2019, 2020, and 2022 to study the relationship between schooling and interest income. We compare linear and nonlinear specifications, including interaction terms, while accounting for other income, demographic characteristics, employment, region, and survey year. Across specifications, greater educational attainment is generally associated with higher interest income. Interest income captures only one component of investment returns, and the observational design limits causal interpretation.

Predicting and Understanding Bike Usage Patterns Based on Temperature and Time-Based Predictor

Collaborative research project

Using hourly observations from Washington, D.C.’s bike-sharing system, we examine how weather and time of day predict demand from casual and registered riders. We compare ordinary least squares with lasso and ridge regression to study how regularization affects prediction. The three models have similar adjusted R² values of about 0.68 when fitted to 2011 observations. On the 2012 holdout sample, their mean absolute errors range from 110 to 112 rentals per hour. A log-linear model that allows the hourly demand pattern to differ between working and nonworking days reduces the error to about 96 rentals per hour, although peaks remain difficult to predict.
